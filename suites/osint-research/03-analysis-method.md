# OSINT Research — analysis method

## 1. Build an answerable claim map

Rewrite the question into atomic propositions. “Is the company credible?” combines identity, licensing, capacity, track record, and honesty into an undefined judgment. Replace it with claims such as “the identified entity held the stated license on the relevant date” or “the stated workforce count is supported by an identified measurement.” Assign each claim a relevance explanation and the minimum evidence needed. Keep a separate section for contextual facts that do not decide the question.

Do not begin with a verdict and search only for support. For each material proposition, write one disconfirming query and one plausible alternative interpretation before collecting evidence. Prioritize the proposition that would most change the recommendation. If license status would decide whether the proposed service is available, confirming a long list of minor awards adds little value while that status remains unresolved.

## 2. Resolve the subject before merging records

Compare stable identifiers first: registration number, issuing jurisdiction, docket party identifier, or authoritative domain association. Then compare names, dates, address history, and linked filings. Record exact agreements and disagreements. Do not assign a universal weighted identity score; the evidentiary value of an identifier depends on its issuer and the possibility of reuse or transcription error.

A material identifier conflict suspends the merge unless there is evidence of a name change, reincorporation, merger, or documented clerical correction. A nearly identical name with a different legal identifier remains a different candidate. Preserve it in coverage notes so a reviewer knows it was considered. Shared brand ownership is a separate proposition from legal-entity identity.

## 3. Collect with a query and coverage log

For each claim, record source family, query text, variants, filters, language, date limits, result pages examined, and inaccessible sources. Use primary records first when available, then accountable secondary reporting to discover additional records or perspectives. Do not assume a primary record independently verifies every statement contained within it; filings can reproduce the subject's representations.

Stop collecting redundant copies when their lineage is established. Instead seek a different observation process: a regulator register, customer filing, court record, dataset, or independently conducted interview. A search can stop provisionally when the decisive propositions have adequate evidence, contradictions have been examined, and additional collection is unlikely to change the answer within the stated scope. Record that stopping rationale. “The model found enough links” is not an adequate rationale.

## 4. Extract claims without losing qualifiers

For each item, capture the exact relevant locator and paraphrase the narrow proposition. Preserve “alleged,” “proposed,” “as of,” “unaudited,” and “subject to appeal.” Separate an allegation from an adjudicated fact, a press announcement from a completed event, and an intended acquisition from a closed acquisition. Where a source uses imprecise numbers, do not add decimal precision.

If a document reports a number, identify unit, population, geography, time period, gross/net basis, and method. A workforce figure may mean headcount, full-time equivalents, contractors, or group-wide staff. A claim of “240 people” cannot be compared mechanically to “190 full-time employees” without reconciling definitions. Record both and the missing bridge.

## 5. Build the chronology

Order events by event date while retaining publication and retrieval dates. Use separate rows for later corrections, appeals, restatements, and status changes. An undated claim belongs in an undated bucket; do not borrow the website's footer year. For partial dates, retain the precision actually known and avoid artificial day-level ordering.

A chronology should explain which facts were knowable at the decision cutoff. A later investigation can inform a present review of a past event, but it must not be described as information available to the original decision maker. If the task is historical, create a contemporaneous view and a later-knowledge view explicitly.

## 6. Evaluate support and contradictions

Classify each claim as SUPPORTED, CONTRADICTED, UNRESOLVED, or IDENTITY UNRESOLVED. SUPPORTED means the narrow claim is supported within the documented scope; it is not a permanent truth label. CONTRADICTED means a relevant source opposes the claim; it does not necessarily establish deception. UNRESOLVED covers insufficient, conflicting, inaccessible, or mismatched evidence. Identity uncertainty has its own state so it cannot be mistaken for merely weak corroboration.

Count independent supporting source families as a descriptive field. Do not mechanically convert two sources into HIGH confidence. One definitive record may outweigh ten copied summaries; two independent sources can repeat the same error. Explain authority, directness, independence, timing, and consistency in words. Where material sources disagree, identify which difference could resolve the conflict and whether it is actually available.

## 7. Quantify coverage transparently

Calculate reviewed planned searches divided by total planned searches, with access-limited searches retained in the denominator. This is workflow coverage, not search recall. Calculate unique source families from upstream lineage, not the number of URLs. Count material claims by their current assessment state; these counts must sum to the total material claims. A claim with two contradictions is still one claim.

Do not calculate an overall “truth percentage.” A supported legal identity and unresolved license are not interchangeable units of credibility. A document-count chart can show collection activity, but it must not imply stronger evidence merely because one proposition has more pages. Put decision-critical gaps ahead of source-volume metrics.

## 8. Draft the conclusion and next research request

Lead with the answer to the bounded question, followed by the decisive evidence and unresolved limitation. Write the strongest alternative interpretation fairly. State what evidence would reverse the conclusion and where it could reasonably be obtained. Rank next steps by decision impact, feasibility, and source authority, not by how easy they are to search.

For publication-oriented work, recheck identity and procedural status immediately before finalizing an adverse statement. Remove irrelevant personal details and speculative associations. The output is an evidence assessment for human review; it does not publish itself or contact the subject. A reviewer should be able to reproduce the logic without accepting the assistant's authority.
