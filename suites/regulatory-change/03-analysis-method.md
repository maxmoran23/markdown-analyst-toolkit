# Analysis method — Regulatory Change and Obligation Mapping

## 1. Define the research boundary

State as-of date, jurisdictions, authority types, covered activities, included publication period, and source channels inspected. Distinguish a supplied-source review from a search intended to identify new developments. Even a broad search is not automatically complete. Document retrieval failures, language limitations, inaccessible annexes, and sources not covered.

Build the instrument register before writing a narrative. Deduplicate repeated coverage of the same instrument using issuer, instrument ID, date, and version. Preserve amendments and corrections as linked changes rather than deleting the earlier version. Multiple news stories about one rule are not multiple regulatory changes.

## 2. Establish status and chronology

Read the action/status designation and operative date provisions. Separate publication from effectiveness, applicability, compliance, comment, reporting-period, and sunset dates. A final instrument may not yet be effective; an effective instrument may have a later compliance deadline; a provision may be effective immediately while another is delayed. Record each relationship explicitly.

Do not calculate a legal deadline from a generic rulemaking heuristic when the actual text states it. If the source specifies a period such as60 days after publication, record the anchor date, counting convention, relevant exceptions, and legal review of the calculation. If the period is ambiguous or a holiday rule matters, leave the legal deadline unresolved until reviewed. The worked example uses explicit dates to avoid pretending to resolve such questions.

Check for subsequent correction, delay, withdrawal, replacement, or relevant stay within the research boundary. “Final” does not mean immutable. A direct-final instrument may have conditions; inspect them rather than assuming it remains operative. A proposal's comment deadline is a consultation milestone, not an implementation obligation.

## 3. Extract atomic requirements

For each provision identify who must do what, to which activity or population, under which condition, by what time, with which exception or transition. Split combined sentences where separate actions have different owners or evidence. Do not split one requirement so finely that reporting counts imply dozens of independent obligations for a single control step.

Compare prior and revised requirements using a change type: new, amended, clarified, removed, delayed, or unchanged context. Explain whether the change affects scope, action, data, timing, reporting, retention, governance, or evidence. A press release emphasizing a topic does not necessarily add a new requirement. A definition change can alter many downstream provisions even when their wording is unchanged.

Preserve limitations of interpretation. If the phrase is ambiguous, set out plausible readings and the facts or legal interpretation needed to resolve them. The assistant can structure the question and cite the text; it does not choose the institution's legal position without an authorized human determination.

## 4. Map applicability

Use a fact-to-provision table. Test jurisdiction, entity category, regulated activity, product, customer or transaction scope, threshold facts, exemptions, and transition conditions. For every gate mark True, False, or Unknown with evidence. A definitive exclusion requires supported facts and a clear textual basis. An unknown licensing or activity fact cannot become False for convenience.

Applicability is In scope when relevant conditions are supported; Out of scope when a supported exclusion or failed necessary condition applies; Conditional when the result depends on a known future or contingent fact; Unresolved when current facts or interpretation are missing. The proposed CH01 example remains Conditional even though its topic could affect the generic activity profile, because the instrument is not final. This is separate from status, which remains PROPOSED.

Avoid treating one broad jurisdiction label as sufficient. An instrument from a jurisdiction can apply by activity, entity, location, or other factors. The suite does not supply those legal tests; retrieve them from the specific authority and obtain appropriate review. A source's title or an organization's self-description is not a substitute for the governing definition.

## 5. Map requirements to controls and evidence

For each applicable or planning provision connect the requirement to an existing control, policy, data field, system behavior, training element, report, or governance step. Use many-to-many relationships where needed: one requirement may require several controls; one control may support several provisions. Preserve obligation IDs so counts do not inflate when one requirement maps to multiple owners.

Assess design coverage, implementation, testing, and operating evidence separately. A policy draft can show intent. A deployed configuration can show a build. A test can show performance on a defined test set. A record from actual operation can support execution. None by itself proves complete compliance. State the specific evidence supporting the readiness status.

Record gaps as absent design, build incomplete, test not performed, test failed, operational evidence missing, or applicability unresolved. An owner saying “done” is a Reported completion claim until inspected evidence supports it. The worked example deliberately contrasts a missing execution artifact with a supported training record.

## 6. Prioritize without a misleading average

Use status, applicability, consequence, verified deadline, remaining work, dependencies, and uncertainty to prioritize. An effective in-scope provision with a passed compliance date and missing evidence warrants prompt review. A proposal may deserve planning attention without becoming an overdue compliance obligation. An unverified near-term report may warrant urgent source confirmation because delay in verification itself could affect planning.

Do not average critical unresolved items into a readiness percentage. Report counts by legal status and readiness stage. If a coverage metric is used, define its denominator as a specific mapped set. “One of two effective mapped requirements has verified fixture evidence” is supportable. “50% compliant” is not established because applicability completeness, sufficiency of evidence, and legal interpretation remain separate.

A deadline distance measured in calendar days is a scheduling metric, not a legal conclusion about breach. A passed date plus missing evidence indicates an evidence gap that needs review. A confirmed missed required act would be a different, stronger observation. Preserve that distinction in severity and claim class.

## 7. Build a human implementation proposal

For every proposed action name an accountable role, deliverable, dependency, acceptance evidence, proposed internal milestone, and final reviewer. Keep internal target dates separate from legal deadlines. If the internal target changes, the legal deadline does not change with it. A plan that is feasible only by assuming an unverified extension must show that dependency prominently.

Define verification before closure: updated text approved, system behavior tested, data reconciled, training evidenced, required operation observed, and relevant exceptions resolved. The appropriate evidence depends on the actual provision. Use fresh post-change records where continuing operation matters. A completed checklist without underlying support does not establish implementation.

## 8. Preserve a change history

At the next manual review, compare source versions and register rows by stable IDs. Record changed dates, status transitions, applicability decisions, evidence additions, and reviewer dispositions. Do not overwrite a former proposed date with a final date without preserving the chronology. If a proposal is withdrawn, retain it as WITHDRAWN and cancel only planning actions that depended exclusively on it, subject to human review.

The result is an evidence-led register for the stated scope and date. It is not a legal opinion, a complete universe of obligations, or authority to send regulatory submissions, commit resources, or change institutional policy.
